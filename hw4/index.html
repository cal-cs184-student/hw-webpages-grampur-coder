Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

In this section, I experiment with the numerical integration simulation by modifying various physical parameters to observe their effects on the cloth's behavior from a starting drop to its final resting state.

1. Effects of Varying the Spring Constant ($k_s$)

The spring constant dictates the stiffness of the structural, shearing, and bending springs, determining the restorative force they apply when stretched.

  • Very Low $k_s$: The cloth behaves like a highly elastic material, such as rubber or Spandex. Because the springs are weak, they exert very little force to maintain their rest length. The weight of the cloth overcomes these springs, causing the cloth to stretch dramatically and sag far down before coming to rest.
  • High $k_s$: The cloth acts like a stiff, heavy fabric like canvas or denim. The strong restorative forces resist deformation, causing the cloth to quickly fold into shape. Its resting state looks tightly held together with minimal stretching.

2. Effects of Varying Density

Density directly scales the mass of each point particle in the simulation. This impacts the total downward gravitational force ($F = mg$) acting on the cloth.

  • Low Density: The cloth is incredibly light. The lower mass means less gravitational force pulls down on the system.
  • High Density: The cloth acts heavy. The immense mass causes gravity to pull down with much greater force.

3. Effects of Varying Damping

Damping simulates the loss of kinetic energy in the system over time, acting as a substitute for air resistance and internal friction.

  • Low Damping: The system retains almost all of its kinetic energy. The cloth swings back and forth chaotically and ripples travel through the fabric. It takes a significantly long time to finally settle.
  • High Damping: The system's kinetic energy is sapped away almost instantly. The cloth falls sluggishly—as if dropping through a thick fluid like molasses—and comes to a dead stop almost immediately without any extra swinging or bouncing.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Implementation Overview

Sphere Collision: To handle sphere collisions,I check if the distance from the point mass to the sphere's origin is less than or equal to the radius. If an intersection occurs, I compute the tangent point on the sphere's surface by extending a vector from the origin through the point mass. I then apply a correction vector to move the point mass to this surface point, then i scale by a friction factor to simulate the cloth dragging against the object.

Plane Collision: To handle plane collisions, I check if a point mass crossed the plane boundary during the previous timestep. If a crossing occurred, I calculate the intersection point, offset it slightly above the surface on the side the mass originated from, and apply the correction vector to rest the mass on the plane.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Implementation Overview

To handle self collisions I implemented spatial hashing to avoid the $O(n^2)$ cost of checking every point mass against every other point mass. At each timestep build a spatial map that partitions the 3D space into discrete bounding boxes. I hashed each point mass's position to find its corresponding box and store it in the map. Then, for every point mass,I only check for collisions against other point masses that share the same 3D bounding box. If two point masses are closer than 2 * thickness, I compute a correction vector to push the current point mass apart from its neighbor. Finally, I average all correction vectors for a given point mass and scale it down by simulation_steps to ensure smooth, stable position updates.

Part 5: Shaders

Shaders explained

A shader program is like an isolated program that runs in parallel on the GPU to execute specific sections of the graphics rendering pipeline. It is fast because it processes many vertices or pixels simultaneously.

Vertex shaders and fragment shaders work together to create lighting and material effects. The vertex shader runs first, it applies transformations to the 3D geometry. It also computes per vertex attributes like normals and UV coordinates, passing them forward as varyings. These varyings are interpolated across the triangles using barycentric coordinates and fed into the fragment shader. The fragment shader then takes these interpolated values and computes the final color for each individual pixel based on lighting equations and texture samples.

Blinn-Phong Shading Model

The Blinn-Phong shading model is a computationally efficient way to simulate how light interacts with surfaces it results in realistic highlights and shading. It combines 3 distinct lighting components to calculate the final color:

  • Ambient Light: Cconstant baseline illumination applied to all objects, ensuring shadows are not just black.
  • Diffuse Light: This simulates matte surfaces. The intensity of the light depends on the angle between the surface normal and the light direction. It is brightest when the light hits the surface directly.
  • Specular Light: Simulates shiny reflections. Calculates the intensity based on the angle between the surface normal and the halfway vector. This creates sharp, bright highlights.

Custom Texture Mapping

Bump Mapping vs. Displacement Mapping

Bump Mapping modifies the normal vectors of an object during the fragment shading stage. It creates the illusion of bumps and ridges through lighting calculations, even though the underlying physical geometry remains perfectly smooth.

Displacement Mapping goes a step further. In the vertex shader, it moves the physical vertex positions along their normal vectors based on the height map. This changes the geometry of the mesh, causing actual silhouettes that matches the texture.

Effects of Mesh Coarseness on Displacement Mapping:

Because Displacement Mapping moves vertices it is dependent on the density. On a coarse sphere (-o 16 -a 16), there are not enough vertices to smoothly capture the details of the height map, resulting in a blocky appearance. On a fine sphere (-o 128 -a 128), there is enough density to smoothly displace the geometry, resulting in a more realistic 3D surface.).
